5.1 Glaucoma & Intraocular Pressure (14 Unique Cards = 20 Exam Questions) 20 Questions Total
💡 Concise Study Review & Exam Concepts (Glaucoma)
  • Intraocular Pressure (IOP) & Tonometry:
    • Normal IOP Range: 10–21 mmHg.
    • Measurement: Measured by Tonometry; the most accurate method (Gold Standard) is Goldmann Applanation Tonometry. (Pachymetry = corneal thickness; Gonioscopy = AC angle; Ophthalmoscopy = fundus).
    • Glaucoma Definition: Progressive optic neuropathy due to increased IOP causing characteristic optic disc cupping and visual field defects.
  • Primary Open-Angle Glaucoma (POAG / Chronic Simple Glaucoma):
    • Risk Factors: Age > 50 years, Black race, Positive family history, High myopia, and Diabetes.
    • Visual Field Defects in POAG: Paracentral scotoma, Seidel's scotoma, Arcuate (Bjerrum) scotoma, Roenne's nasal step, and Annular (ring) scotoma / tubular vision. (Exam Trap: Binasal quadrantanopia or hemianopia is a neuro-ophthalmic chiasmal/tract defect and NEVER occurs in POAG!).
    • Medical Treatment: First-line in Thamar exam keys is topical Beta-blocker (Timolol) (or Prostaglandin analogs). Contraindications to Beta-blockers: Asthma / COPD, Congestive Heart Failure / Heart block, and Brittle Diabetes (masks hypoglycemia).
  • Primary Acute Angle-Closure (Congestive) Glaucoma (AACG):
    • Mechanism: Pupillary block leading to occlusion of the trabecular drainage angle by the peripheral iris in an eye with a shallow anterior chamber (hypermetropic eye).
    • Clinical Picture: Acute painful loss of vision (down to Hand Movements) + redness, profuse lacrimation, colored halos around lights, stony-hard high IOP, shallow AC, and a vertically oval, mid-dilated fixed pupil (NOT a pinpoint constricted pupil!).
    • Management & Drug Contraindication:
      • First-line acute medical IOP reduction: Systemic/IV Carbonic Anhydrase Inhibitor (Acetazolamide) + Hyperosmotic Mannitol + Topical Beta-blocker + Pilocarpine (miotic).
      • Prophylactic & Definitive Treatment of Choice for the **Fellow (Other) Eye**: Nd:YAG Laser Peripheral Iridotomy (LPI)!
      • Strictly Contraindicated Drug: Atropine (and all mydriatics) — dilates the pupil, crowding the iris into the angle and worsening the attack!
  • Congenital Glaucoma (Buphthalmos): Presents in an infant (e.g., 1-month-old boy) with the classic triad of epiphora (lacrimation), photophobia, blepharospasm + large hazy/cloudy cornea (>13 mm) due to Haab's striae and corneal edema.

Correct Answer: D (Binasal quadrantanopia).
Rationale: Retinal nerve fiber bundle damage in POAG causes paracentral, arcuate (Bjerrum), annular (double arcuate), and Roenne's nasal step defects. Binasal quadrantanopia is a neurological chiasmal defect.
Batch 22 Midterm - Q13
8. First-line treatment of open-angle glaucoma (OAG) is:
Correct Answer: A (Beta-blocker).
Rationale: Among the given choices, topical Beta-blockers (e.g., Timolol 0.5%, which decreases aqueous humor secretion by the ciliary epithelium) are the first-line medical therapy for POAG.
Batch 23 Final - Q21
9. Topical/systemic beta blockers are relatively contraindicated in patients with:
Correct Answer: D (All of the above).
Rationale: Non-selective beta-blockers (Timolol) block β2 receptors in bronchi (causing fatal bronchospasm in asthma/COPD), block β1 receptors in the heart (worsening heart failure/bradycardia), and mask hypoglycemic tachycardia in brittle diabetics.

10. The mechanism of primary angle-closure glaucoma is:
Correct Answer: B (Occlusion of trabecular angle).
Rationale: In PACG, relative pupillary block bows the peripheral iris forward (iris bombé) to physically occlude the trabecular meshwork drainage angle, causing a rapid spike in IOP.

11. An adult patient presents with acute painful loss of vision, red eye, lacrimation, colored halos, increased IOP, and shallow anterior chamber angle. The probable diagnosis is:
Correct Answer: D (Acute closed-angle glaucoma). (Option D in Batch 24 Midterm; Option A in Batch 22 Final).
Rationale: Severe ocular pain + red eye + colored halos (from corneal epithelial edema) + decreased VA + high IOP + shallow AC is the classic presentation of an acute congestive angle-closure glaucoma attack.
Batch 20 Final - Q17
12. Primary acute closed angle glaucoma is characterized by:
Correct Answer: A (Vision diminished to hand movement).
Rationale: In an acute congestive attack, corneal edema and optic nerve ischemia reduce vision profoundly to Hand Movements (HM). The pupil is mid-dilated vertically oval (NOT pinpoint!), and optic disc cupping only develops in chronic neglected stages, not during the first acute attack.
Batch 22 Final - Q6
13. The first-line treatment of acute congestive glaucoma is:
Correct Answer: B (Carbonic anhydrase inhibitor).
Rationale: Immediate systemic/IV Acetazolamide (Carbonic anhydrase inhibitor) + hyperosmotic agents are given first-line in an acute attack to rapidly lower IOP below 40 mmHg (because when IOP is >50 mmHg, the iris sphincter is ischemic and paralyzed, so Pilocarpine cannot work until IOP is lowered first!).

14. Prophylactic treatment of choice for the fellow (other) eye in angle-closure glaucoma is:
Correct Answer: B (YAG Laser iridotomy).
Rationale: Primary angle closure is a bilateral anatomical predisposition (50–75% risk of fellow eye attack within 5 years); prophylactic Nd:YAG Laser Peripheral Iridotomy (LPI) in the fellow eye is mandatory to bypass pupillary block.
Batch 23 Final - Q6
15. Which drug is contraindicated in acute primary angle-closure glaucoma?
Correct Answer: B (Atropine).
Rationale: Mydriatics/cycloplegics like Atropine dilate the pupil and bunch up the peripheral iris tissue into the narrow drainage angle, precipitating or worsening angle closure.
5.2 Retina & Chemical Ocular Emergencies (12 Unique Cards = 15 Exam Questions) 15 Questions Total
💡 Concise Study Review & Exam Concepts (Retina & Emergencies)
  • Central Retinal Artery Occlusion (CRAO): Presents in a cardiac/carotid/hypertensive patient with sudden, painless, profound monocular loss of vision (down to No Perception of Light / Counting Fingers) + RAPD + pale milky retina with a macular Cherry-Red Spot (intact choroidal glow seen through thin avascular foveola).
  • Diabetic & Hypertensive Retinopathy:
    • Diabetic Retinopathy (DR): Neovascularization (hallmark of Proliferative DR) occurs due to Retinal Ischemia (VEGF release). Hard exudates are composed mainly of Lipids leaking from abnormal capillaries.
    • Hypertensive Retinopathy: **1st / Earliest sign** is generalized Arteriolar Narrowing (vasoconstriction). Typical signs include **Cotton-wool spots** (nerve fiber layer micro-infarcts) and **Hard exudates** (macular star). **4th Stage (Malignant) Hypertensive Retinopathy** is defined by the presence of Papilledema (Optic disc swelling)!
    • Roth Spots (White-centered retinal hemorrhages): Non-specific; seen in Subacute Bacterial Endocarditis, Leukemia/Anemia, Hypertension, and Diabetes Mellitus.
  • Retinal Detachment (RD) & Retinitis Pigmentosa (RP):
    • Retinal Detachment: Completely Painless; preceded by premonitory Flashes of light (Photopsia) and Floaters, followed by sudden visual loss described as a "black curtain / veil falling" across the field of vision.
    • Retinitis Pigmentosa: Dystrophy of retinal rods presenting classically with Night Blindness (Nyctalopia) + tunnel vision (ring scotoma) + fundus triad of **bone-spicule pigmentation, attenuated arterioles, and waxy disc pallor**.
  • Chemical Injury to the Eye (Alkali / Acid Burn): True ophthalmic emergency; the **immediate first-line management** before any test or referral is copious irrigation with normal saline or plain tap water!
Batch 20 Midterm - Q5
16. A cardiac patient complains of sudden painless loss of vision in one eye with no perception of light. The possible diagnosis is:
Correct Answer: D (Central retinal artery occlusion).
Rationale: Cardiac emboli (from atrial fibrillation or valvular disease) lodging in the central retinal artery cause instantaneous, painless, profound monocular blindness (often down to No PL).
Batch 22 Final - Q28
17. Cherry red spot is seen in:
Correct Answer: A (Central retinal artery occlusion — CRAO).
Rationale: In CRAO, ischemic Cloudy swelling whitens the ganglion cell layer of the posterior pole, whereas the thin foveola (which lacks ganglion cells) allows the normal red choroidal reflex to shine through as a Cherry-Red Spot.
Batch 23 Midterm - Q5
18. In diabetic retinopathy, neovascularization occurs due to:
Correct Answer: B (Retinal ischemia).
Rationale: Capillary non-perfusion and retinal hypoxia/ischemia stimulate the ischemic retina to secrete Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor (VEGF), driving proliferative retinal neovascularization.
Batch 23 Final - Q14
19. Hard exudates in the retina are composed mainly of:
Correct Answer: B (Lipids).
Rationale: Hard exudates are yellow, waxy intraretinal deposits of lipoproteins and lipid-laden macrophages located in the outer plexiform layer due to chronic microvascular leakage.
Batch 23 Midterm - Q13
20. The first sign of hypertensive retinopathy is:
Correct Answer: D (Arteriolar narrowing).
Rationale: The earliest autoregulatory response of retinal arterioles to systemic hypertension is generalized/focal vasoconstriction (Grade 1 arteriolar narrowing).
Batch 23 Final - Q15
21. Which of the following are typical signs of hypertensive retinopathy?
Correct Answer: C (Both A and B).
Rationale: Grade 3 hypertensive retinopathy features flame-shaped hemorrhages, cotton-wool spots (NFL ischemia), and hard exudates (often arranged as a macular star).
Batch 20 Final - Q20
22. Which of the following is seen in the 4th stage of hypertensive retinopathy:
Correct Answer: B (Papilledema).
Rationale: In the Keith-Wagener-Barker classification, Stage 4 (malignant) hypertensive retinopathy is defined by the addition of bilateral optic disc swelling (Papilledema) to Stage 3 changes.
Batch 23 Final - Q20
23. Roth spots (white-centered retinal hemorrhages) can be seen in:
Correct Answer: D (All of the above).
Rationale: Roth spots (retinal hemorrhages with a central white fibrin-platelet plug or focal ischemia) occur in subacute bacterial endocarditis, leukemia, severe anemia, hypertension, and diabetes.

25. Associated with night blindness:
Correct Answer: A (Retinitis pigmentosa).
Rationale: Retinitis pigmentosa is a hereditary rod-cone photoreceptor dystrophy in which early degeneration of retinal rods causes Nyctalopia (night blindness) as the earliest hallmark symptom.
Batch 23 Final - Q36

Correct Answer: B (Copious irrigation with saline/water).
Rationale: Immediate, continuous, copious irrigation with normal saline, Ringer's lactate, or plain tap water for at least 15–30 minutes until pH normalizes (7.0–7.4) is the single most critical sight-saving intervention in chemical burns.

Table 2: Master Diagnostic Tests & Pathognomonic Clinical Signs ("Buzzword Decoder")

🔬 Diagnostic Tests & Instruments

  • Corneal Topography (Pentacam): Gold standard for Keratoconus.
  • Pachymetry: Measures Corneal Thickness.
  • Specular Microscopy: Examines Corneal Endothelium.
  • Fluorescein 2% Stain (Green): Sure sign of Corneal Ulcer (denuded stroma).
  • Seidel Test (+ve): Detects Corneal Perforation / Aqueous Leak.
  • Schirmer Test: Diagnoses Dry Eye (Xerophthalmia).
  • Goldmann Applanation Tonometry: Most accurate measurement of IOP.
  • Gonioscopy: Examines the Anterior Chamber Drainage Angle.
  • Hirschberg Test: Estimates the Angle of Squint (Strabismus).
  • Ishihara Plates: Tests Color Vision (optic nerve function).
  • B-Scan Ultrasonography: Evaluates posterior segment when media is opaque (Vitreous hemorrhage / Mature cataract) & extraocular muscles in Graves' disease.

🎯 Pathognomonic Signs & Triads

  • Scissoring Reflex + Oil-Droplet + Munson's Sign + Fleischer (Iron) Ring + Vogt's Striae: Keratoconus.
  • Cobblestone Giant Papillae + Horner-Trantas Dots + Ropy White Discharge + Shield Ulcer: Vernal Keratoconjunctivitis (VKC).
  • Arlt's Line + Herbert's Pits + Superior Pannus + H.P. Inclusion Bodies: Trachoma.
  • Macerated Skin at Canthi + Diplobacilli: Angular Conjunctivitis (Moraxella).
  • Bitot's Foamy Spot + Keratomalacia + Night Blindness: Vitamin A Deficiency.
  • Aqueous Flare (Earliest) + KPs (Diagnostic) + Miotic Festooned Pupil + Koeppe's Nodules: Anterior Uveitis (Iridocyclitis).
  • Rosette Cataract + Vossius Ring + 'D'-Shaped Pupil (Iridodialysis): Blunt Ocular Trauma.
  • Sunflower Cataract + Kayser-Fleischer Ring: Wilson's Disease (Copper).
  • Pain on Eye Movement + Unilateral Visual Loss + RAPD (Marcus Gunn Pupil): Optic Neuritis.
  • Bilateral Disc Swelling + Enlarged Blind Spot + Normal Early Vision (6/6): Papilledema (Raised ICP).
  • Ptosis + Miosis (Anisocoria worse in Dark) + Anhidrosis: Horner Syndrome.
  • Light-Near Dissociation (Reacts to Near, NOT to Light): Argyll Robertson Pupil (Neurosyphilis).
  • Ptosis + Eye Down & Out + Dilated Fixed Pupil: Surgical CN III Palsy (PCom Aneurysm).
