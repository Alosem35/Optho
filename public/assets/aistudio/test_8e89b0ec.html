1.1 Introduction, Basic Anatomy & Ocular Physiology 5 Questions
💡 Concise Study Review & Exam Concepts (Introduction)
  • Light Control vs. Light Reception: The Iris acts as the adjustable diaphragm controlling the amount of light entering the eye (via sphincter and dilator pupillae muscles), whereas Photoreceptor cells (Rods & Cones) are located in the outer neural Retina. Photoreceptors are completely absent at the Optic Disc (Blind spot), and Cones are maximally concentrated at the avascular Fovea Centralis.
  • Ocular Chambers & Aqueous Pathway: The eye is divided into the Anterior Segment (Anterior Chamber = in front of iris; Posterior Chamber = behind the iris and in front of the lens/zonules) and the Posterior Segment (behind the lens, containing the vitreous). Aqueous humor is secreted by the Ciliary processes (pars plicata) into the Posterior Chamber → flows through the pupil into the Anterior Chamber → drains mainly (~85–90%) via the conventional pathway through the Trabecular Meshwork into Schlemm's Canal (and 10–15% via the uveoscleral outflow).
  • Routine Diagnostic Mydriatic: Short-acting antimuscarinic Tropicamide (0.5%–1%) is the drug of choice for routine fundus examination because of its rapid onset (20–30 min) and short duration (4–6 hours), unlike Atropine which lasts 7–14 days.
Batch 22 Final - Q11
1. The part of the eye that controls the amount of light entering:
Correct Answer: A (Iris).
Rationale: The iris regulates pupillary aperture size via the parasympathetic sphincter pupillae (miosis in bright light) and sympathetic dilator pupillae (mydriasis in dim light), thereby controlling the amount of light reaching the retina.
Batch 22 Final - Q12
2. The part of the eye where photoreceptor cells are found:
Correct Answer: A (Retina).
Rationale: Rods and cones are the outer neurosensory photoreceptor cells of the retina that convert light energy into electrical neural impulses (phototransduction).

Correct Answer: B (After iris).
Rationale: The posterior chamber is the narrow aqueous-filled space located immediately behind (after) the iris and in front of the crystalline lens and zonular fibers. (Behind the lens is the vitreous cavity/posterior segment).

Correct Answer: B (Schlemm's canal).
Rationale: About 85–90% of aqueous humor drains through the conventional trabecular pathway (Trabecular meshwork → Schlemm's canal → Aqueous veins → Episcleral veins).
Batch 22 Final - Q5

Correct Answer: B (Tropicamide).
Rationale: Tropicamide (0.5–1%) is the standard diagnostic mydriatic for ophthalmoscopy due to its fast onset (20 min) and short duration (~4–6 hrs), avoiding prolonged cycloplegia.
1.2 Conjunctiva & Dry Eye Syndrome (Xerosis) 18 Questions
💡 Concise Study Review & Exam Concepts (Conjunctiva & Dry Eye)
  • Viral Conjunctivitis: Most commonly caused by Adenovirus. Look for: child/adult with recent fever & sore throat + excessive watery lacrimation + follicles in lower palpebral conjunctiva + tender preauricular lymphadenopathy.
  • Bacterial & Neonatal Conjunctivitis:
    • Ophthalmia Neonatorum: Most dangerous causative organism is Neisseria gonorrhoeae (Gonococci) because it penetrates intact corneal epithelium causing rapid perforation.
    • Membranous Conjunctivitis: Caused by Corynebacterium diphtheriae; true membrane formation + purulent secretion + cicatrization/paralysis leading to squint.
    • Eye Bandage Contraindication: Patching the eye is strictly contraindicated in Purulent / Mucopurulent Conjunctivitis (retains pus and raises temperature, causing corneal melting).
  • Trachoma (Chlamydia trachomatis A–C):
    • WHO Classification (FISTO): TF (Follicular), TI (Intense), TS (Scarring / Arlt's line), TT (Trichiasis), CO (Corneal Opacity). Pannus and Trantas spots are NOT in the WHO FISTO classification!
    • Treatment Fact: Medical treatment (Oral Azithromycin / Topical Tetracycline) is effective in active stages; surgery is only needed for cicatricial TT/CO.
  • Vernal Keratoconjunctivitis (Spring Catarrh / VKC): Bilateral recurrent allergic inflammation in young boys during hot weather with intense itching and thick white ropy (mucoid) discharge (NOT mucopurulent and NOT pseudomembranous!).
    • Palpebral sign: Large flat-topped Cobblestone (giant) papillae on upper tarsus.
    • Bulbar/Limbal sign: Gelatinous nodules with chalky white Horner-Trantas dots (degenerated eosinophils).
    • Corneal complication: Shield ulcer & Keratoconus.
  • Phlyctenular Conjunctivitis: Delayed Type IV cell-mediated hypersensitivity to endogenous antigens (Tuberculoprotein or Staphylococcal antigens).
  • Pterygium: Non-neoplastic degenerative fibrovascular wing caused by chronic Ultraviolet (UV) rays exposure (NOT a benign tumor and NOT linked to immunocompromise!).
  • Dry Eye & Xerosis:
    • Sjögren's Syndrome: Autoimmune triad of Dry Eyes (Xerophthalmia / Keratoconjunctivitis sicca) + Dry Mouth (Xerostomia) + Rheumatoid Arthritis.
    • Bitot's Spots: Foamy triangular keratinized plaques on bulbar conjunctiva caused by Vitamin A deficiency.
    • Diagnosis & Treatment: Diagnosed by Schirmer Test (<5 mm in 5 min); mild dry eye is treated first-line with Topical lubricants (artificial tears).

1.3 The Cornea (Keratitis, Ulcers, Keratoconus & Opacities) 18 Questions
💡 Concise Study Review & Exam Concepts (The Cornea)
  • Corneal Nutrition: Supplied by Air (oxygen), Aqueous humor (glucose), and Perilimbal capillaries. Vitreous humor is NOT a source of nutrition to the cornea!
  • Intact Epithelium Invaders: Only 4 bacteria can penetrate an intact corneal epithelium: Corynebacterium diphtheriae, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, N. meningitidis, and Haemophilus.
  • Specific Bacterial & Protozoan Ulcers:
    • Commonest cause of Typical Hypopyon Corneal Ulcer (Ulcus serpens): Streptococcus pneumoniae (Pneumococcus) (linked to chronic dacryocystitis).
    • Commonest bacterium in Contact Lens Wearers: Pseudomonas aeruginosa.
    • Acanthamoeba Keratitis (Contact lens + tap water): Severe pain out of proportion + Ring-shaped corneal stromal infiltrate.
  • Herpes Simplex Keratitis (HSK):
    • Recurrent features: Branching Dendritic ulcer with terminal bulbs, Geographic ulcer (if steroids were misused), and Disciform stromal keratitis. (Superficial punctate keratitis is NOT a feature of recurrent HSK!).
    • Key Diagnostic Sign: Loss of corneal sensation (Corneal hypoesthesia) due to CN V1 damage.
    • Treatment & Contraindication: Treated with Topical Acyclovir 3% ointment. Topical Steroids are STRICTLY CONTRAINDICATED in dendritic ulcer!
  • Corneal Scars, Depositions & Perforation:
    • Leucoma Adherent: Dense white corneal scar with cicatrization and iris incarceration following a perforated ulcer.
    • Seidel Test: Positive test indicates anterior chamber aqueous leakage due to corneal perforation/fistula.
    • Band Keratopathy: Subepithelial deposition of Calcium salts.
    • Salmon Patches: Deep stromal vascularization seen in syphilitic Interstitial Keratitis.
  • Keratoconus (Top Tested Topic — 9 Questions in Batches 20–24!):
    • Pathology: Non-inflammatory central thinning and conical bulging of the cornea presenting in young adults (~20–22 yrs) with gradual visual loss and high irregular myopic astigmatism.
    • Signs: Scissoring red reflex on retinoscopy, Fleischer ring (epithelial iron deposition at cone base), Munson's sign (V-shaped lower lid indentation on down-gaze), and Acute Hydrops (sudden corneal edema due to rupture of Descemet's membrane).
    • Gold Standard Investigation: Corneal Topography.

1.4 Optics & Errors of Refraction 6 Questions
💡 Concise Study Review & Exam Concepts (Errors of Refraction)
  • Primary Cause of Refractive Errors: Change in the anteroposterior Axial Length of the globe (Long globe = Axial Myopia; Short globe = Axial Hypermetropia).
  • Optical Lens Corrections (Memorize!):
    • Myopia (Nearsightedness): Parallel rays focus in front of the retina → corrected with Concave (Minus / Diverging) lenses. High/pathological myopia predisposes to Rhegmatogenous Retinal Detachment and Posterior Staphyloma.
    • Hypermetropia / Hyperopia (Farsightedness): Parallel rays focus behind the retina → corrected with Convex (Plus / Converging) lenses. Predisposes to Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma and Accommodative Esotropia in children.
    • Astigmatism: Corrected with Cylindrical lenses (or rigid contact lenses if irregular).
    • Presbyopia: Age-related loss of accommodation after age 40 → primary optical treatment is Bifocal (or Plus reading) glasses.
  • Unilateral Aphakia (Top Repeated Exam Question!): Can be corrected by PCIOL, ACIOL, Contact lenses, or Refractive surgery, EXCEPT Glasses (Spectacles)! Why? Because +10 D aphakic glasses magnify the retinal image by 25–30% (Aniseikonia), causing intolerable binocular diplopia when the fellow eye is phakic.
Batch 22 Final - Q2
45. The primary cause of refractive errors (myopia, hypermetropia, astigmatism) is:
Correct Answer: B (Change in axial length).
Rationale: Axial length variation (normal ~24 mm; 1 mm change = ~3 D) is the most common anatomical determinant of refractive errors.
Batch 23 Midterm - Q10

Correct Answer: B (Concave lenses).
Rationale: Concave (minus) lenses diverge incoming parallel light rays so the focal point moves backward onto the myopic retina.

2.1 Lacrimal System & The Orbit 5 Questions
💡 Concise Study Review & Exam Concepts (Lacrimal & Orbit)
  • Most Common Cause of Proptosis Worldwide: Thyroid Eye Disease (Graves' Orbitopathy) is the #1 cause of both unilateral and bilateral proptosis in adults.
  • Orbital Cellulitis vs. Preseptal Cellulitis vs. Cavernous Sinus Thrombosis:
    • Orbital Cellulitis: Infection posterior to the orbital septum (usually secondary to ethmoiditis). Presents with painful red eye, eyelid edema, proptosis, and ophthalmoplegia (restricted eye movements) + rapid vision loss if optic nerve is compressed.
    • Complete loss of vision with rapid onset: Occurs in Panophthalmitis, severe Orbital Cellulitis, and Cavernous Sinus Thrombosis.
  • Orbital Pseudotumor: Best described as an idiopathic non-neoplastic inflammatory condition of the orbit presenting with painful proptosis; responds dramatically to systemic steroids.
  • Orbital Floor "Blow-Out" Fracture: Blunt trauma by a round object fractures the inferior orbital wall, entrapping the inferior rectus/oblique muscles and typically causing Diplopia (double vision), enophthalmos, and infraorbital numbness (NOT proptosis or ptosis!).

2.2 The Uveal Tract (Uveitis, Behçet, Rubeosis & Endophthalmitis) 9 Questions
💡 Concise Study Review & Exam Concepts (Uveal Tract)
  • Etiology of Uveitis:
    • Primary / Most Common Overall Cause of Uveitis: Idiopathic (~50%).
    • Most Common Systemic Disease Associated with Acute Anterior Uveitis: HLA-B27 seronegative spondyloarthropathies, specifically Ankylosing Spondylitis.
  • Clinical Features of Acute Iridocyclitis (Anterior Uveitis):
    • Constant Symptom: Diminution of vision (due to aqueous flare, KPs, and ciliary spasm — NOT night blindness!).
    • Granulomatous Uveitis Sign: Koeppe's nodules (small nodules at the pupillary border of the iris) and Busacca's nodules (on iris stroma).
    • Behçet's Disease: Characterized by bilateral recurrent iridocyclitis + recurrent mobile hypopyon + recurrent oral, conjunctival, and genital ulcerations.
  • Treatment of Acute Iridocyclitis:
    • Most Commonly Used Anti-inflammatory Drug: Corticosteroids.
    • Role of Atropine (Cycloplegic/Mydriatic): Relieves pain (paralyzes ciliary muscle spasm) AND prevents/breaks posterior synechiae (adhesions between iris and anterior lens capsule).
  • Rubeosis Iridis (Iris Neovascularization): Caused by severe retinal ischemia, most commonly Proliferative Diabetic Retinopathy AND Central Retinal Vein Occlusion (CRVO) → leads to Neovascular Glaucoma.
  • Endophthalmitis Definition: Purulent inflammation of the internal structures/cavities of the eye (aqueous, vitreous, retina, uvea) sparing the sclera.
Batch 22 Final - Q9
56. The primary cause of uveitis is:
Correct Answer: D (Idiopathic).
Rationale: Roughly 50% of all anterior uveitis cases have no identifiable systemic or infectious cause and are classified as idiopathic.

62. Atropine in the treatment of acute iridocyclitis:
Correct Answer: B (Relieves pain and prevents posterior synechia).

Correct Answer: C (Both).

Batch 20 Final - Q12
64. Endophthalmitis means:
Correct Answer: A (Inflammation of internal structures of the eye).
Rationale: Endophthalmitis is purulent inflammation of the intraocular cavities (aqueous and vitreous) and inner coats (uvea/retina) without scleral or extraocular involvement (inflammation of all eye coats + orbit is Panophthalmitis).
2.3 The Sclera 1 Question
💡 Concise Study Review & Exam Concepts (The Sclera)
  • Blue Sclera: Caused by congenital thinning/abnormal collagen of the sclera allowing dark uveal/choroidal pigment to show through. Classic systemic associations: Osteogenesis Imperfecta and Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome (also Marfan syndrome and Buphthalmos).
  • Scleritis vs. Episcleritis: Scleritis causes severe boring pain that wakes the patient at night and is strongly associated with Rheumatoid Arthritis; Episcleritis is benign, superficial, and blanches with phenylephrine.
  • Ocular Staphylomas: Ectasia of outer fibrous coat lined by uvea. Posterior Staphyloma occurs in Pathological High Myopia and is the only staphyloma with normal IOP.
Batch 23 Final - Q38
65. Blue sclera is a feature of:
Correct Answer: C (Ehlers-Danlos / Osteogenesis imperfecta).
Rationale: Defective Type I/III collagen synthesis in Osteogenesis Imperfecta and Ehlers-Danlos syndrome leads to an abnormally thin, translucent sclera through which the underlying dark choroid is visible as a blue hue.
2.4 The Vitreous Humor 2 Questions
💡 Concise Study Review & Exam Concepts (The Vitreous)
  • Vitreous Hemorrhage:
    • Most Common Cause Overall: Proliferative Diabetic Retinopathy (PDR) (rupture of fragile neovascular vessels into the vitreous cavity). (In young adults, Trauma and Eales' disease are most common).
    • Clinical Presentation: Causes sudden, painless loss of vision (alongside CRAO and Retinal Detachment), floaters, and an absent/dim red reflex with obscured fundus view. Investigated by B-scan ultrasonography.

66. The most common cause of vitreous hemorrhage is:
Correct Answer: C (Proliferative diabetic retinopathy).
Rationale: Fragile retinal neovascularization (NVD/NVE) in Proliferative Diabetic Retinopathy is the leading overall cause of spontaneous vitreous hemorrhage.

Correct Answer: C (Both A and B).
